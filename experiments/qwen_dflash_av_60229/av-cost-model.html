What slowed down adaptive verification, and what it revealed

Qwen3.5-35B-A3B (BF16) with a DFlash drafter (k=7) on B300, SPEED-Bench throughput-1K, vLLM PR #60229. Extended to Qwen3.8-Flash-Next and GLM-5.3-Flash. Steady-state AIPerf harness with validity gates, 3 repeats unless noted.

Summary

  1. The AV slowdown was a symptom of a much larger problem. Every step that contains a prefill exceeds vLLM's 1,024-token CUDA graph capture limit, so it runs fully eagerly and CPU-bound. With a few extra capture sizes the AV penalty disappears, and throughput rises by 25 to 37% on Qwen3.5, up to 96% on Qwen3.8-Flash-Next and up to 55% on GLM-5.3-Flash.
  2. AV's startup cost profile was broken. Its dummy batches pointed the GDN layers at empty states, so the profile skipped the recurrent work and underpriced every multi-token verify step by up to 2.5×. A fix is in a fork draft PR.
  3. Accurate costs help AV at high concurrency, but a well-chosen fixed K is almost as good. Measured cost tables gave +2 to +10% at c128 and −2 to −4% at c1 to c8. Fixed k=5 nearly matched the best AV at every concurrency. Whether per-request adaptivity beats a simple batch-size-keyed K is now an open empirical question.

2 · Cause

Steps with a prefill run eagerly and are CPU-bound

In this closed-loop benchmark the engine alternates between decode-only steps and steps that also prefill new prompts. The two kinds have very different costs:

vLLM captures CUDA graphs only up to max_cudagraph_capture_size, by default 1,024 tokens on Blackwell. Decode-only steps fit and replay full graphs. A step with a ~1,080-token prompt plus decode tokens does not fit, so it runs fully eagerly, not even with piecewise graphs. Such a step takes ~90 ms of wall time for ~19 ms of GPU work. Its cost barely depends on how many prompts it holds (1, 2 or 3 prompts at c128: 87 to 90 ms), and at c128 these steps take about two thirds of the run.

The CPU time is spread over ~1,500 eager kernels at ~60 to 70 µs of CPU each. The driver launch itself is a few µs, the rest is Python and framework work between launches. A py-spy profile puts 55% in Inductor-compiled pieces running without a graph (MoE, linears, norms), 38% in the GDN linear-attention layers (always eager), and 4% in full attention. There is no single hotspot.

This explains the AV penalty: AV saves time only by shortening decode-only steps, while the eager prefill steps that dominate the run cost the same with or without it.

The same gap appears on other hybrid MoE models

We repeated the capture-grid comparison on two newer models with their published B300 recipes, changing only the capture list:

Throughput gain of the extended list (up to 4,096 or 6,144 tokens) over the default, AV off. Qwen3.8 and Qwen3.5 have 3 repeats, GLM 2 to 3.

The grid has to reach the largest mixed step the scheduler produces, roughly one prompt plus max_num_seqs × (1 + k) decode tokens. At c256 with MTP3, one 1,080-token prompt plus 255 × 4 decode tokens is 2,100 tokens, so a grid up to 2,048 tokens barely helps while one up to 4,096 tokens gives +52%. The memory cost is model-specific: 0.4% of KV cache on Qwen3.8 but 7% on GLM-5.3-Flash, so a new default needs a memory bound. Raised upstream as #62556. Related reports: #56975 (GLM-5.3-Flash on GB200), #44806 (Qwen3.5-397B), #60264 (small max_num_seqs).

5 · AV's cost profile

The startup profile skipped GDN recurrent work

AV picks a draft budget per step by maximizing expected accepted tokens per millisecond. The milliseconds come from a cost table profiled at startup with dummy batches. Those dummy batches assigned the GDN layers empty state slots. The GDN decode kernel returns early for an empty source state, so every dummy request with 2 or more query tokens skipped its recurrent computation:

exact shape (requests × query tokens)profiled, empty stateswith valid states
128 × 17.2 ms7.0 ms

512 × 210.5 ms25.9 ms

The eager tail above 4,096 tokens had a related problem: oversized dummy queries also hit an early return. Both fixes are in fork draft PR tomasruizt/vllm#21, rebased onto the community PRs that profile the tail as mixed batches (#54065, #54077). Every AV result above used the broken table.

6 · Does the cost table matter?

Measured costs help at high concurrency, a fixed K is almost as good

With extended capture sizes, we replaced AV's startup table with costs measured from real serving steps, keeping its acceptance estimates and confidence ranking unchanged:

cost tablec1c8c128
fitted from fixed-K runs (drafter k varies)−2.2%−3.7%+10.5%
fitted with drafter k=7 held fixed−2.7%−3.8%+7.1%
direct measured lookup, no fitting+3.1%−0.2%+2.2%
direct lookup, c256 on 2 GPUsc256: −1.1%

Throughput change against AV with its startup table, medians of 3 to 6 paired runs. With better prices AV verifies more drafts at c128 (1.0 → 2.3 per request), so the startup table over-pruned there. But the gains depend on how the table is built, and the low-concurrency regressions show that fitted tables misprice small budgets.

Two limits remain. First, a well-chosen fixed K is nearly as good: k=5 matches or beats AV at c1 and c8 and is within the repeat spread at c128. Second, a token-count table cannot price mixed steps, whose cost depends on how many decode requests and prefill tokens they contain. At c256 such steps carry 64% of AV's decisions. A composition-aware measured cost model is in progress.

So the open question is whether AV's per-request adaptivity is worth its complexity, compared with the batch-size-keyed K schedule vLLM already ships (dynamic speculative decoding, RFC #54749). That needs a direct comparison on a workload where acceptance varies strongly between requests.

7 · Next steps

What we recommend

  1. A capture-grid default that covers mixed steps (#62556). Extend the list to the largest mixed step the scheduler can produce, with sparse spacing, bounded by graph memory as a share of KV cache. Still needed: a dense model and Hopper to show where the gain fades, and an accounting of graph memory (the GLM case). This has by far the largest payoff for users.
  2. Upstream the AV profiling fix (tomasruizt/vllm#21 on top of #54065 and #54077). Every other AV tuning depends on correct costs.
  3. AV versus dynamic K. Compare AV (with the profiling fix) against fixed K and a tuned batch-size-keyed K schedule, on SPEED-Bench and on a workload with heterogeneous acceptance. If AV only matches the best K, the simpler policy is the better product.
  4. Measured, composition-aware costs, only if step 3 shows that adaptivity pays off.

Leads that did not pan out: a suspected host sync in the GDN prefill path (none in the traces, ~1% CPU), caching flashinfer's MoE tactic lookup (at most ~2% end to end, and graphs bypass it anyway), and the MoE padding mask during AV profiling (does not affect the monolithic backend used here).

Appendix

Method and corrections

  • Harness. Fixed request count per cell, no cancellations, throughput and latency over the steady-state window, gates for saturation, queueing, preemptions and errors. AV off and on alternate across two GPUs with a fresh server per repeat. Identical prompt sets across arms.
  • Step-level data. AIPerf per-chunk latencies, nsys, torch profiler, py-spy, and a trace patch that logs each executed step's shape and AV's decisions. Step times from CPU-side gaps are approximate under async scheduling.
  • Earlier claims that turned out wrong. The profile dip was not a slower 1-token kernel path but the empty-state bug. A suspected "fewer prompts share each prefill step" effect remained a weak correlation and is not part of the explanation. An early cost-table dump was distorted by debug logging, one sweep ran unpatched code, and two runs once collided on the same ports. All were detected and rerun.
